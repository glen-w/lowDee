# Teaching the Irish Low D: Concept and Engineering Pathways

A product and engineering brief for software that teaches the Irish low-D whistle to people who do not share a musical background.

The recommendation up front: build a **practice instrument with three front doors**, not another tune jukebox. The durable advantage is low-D-specific teaching (air, octave, hand span, C-natural fingerings, phrase breathing) plus a placement path that sends a classical flutist, a high-D player, and a non-reader down different routes into the same skill graph. Tune libraries are table stakes. Honest listening, and the refusal to grade when the audio is ambiguous, is the product.

---

## 1. What “can play” means

Irish traditional whistle playing is a cluster of skills that notation apps usually collapse into “hit the right pitch at the right time.”

A player who can sit in on low D can:

- Make a stable, in-tune note in both octaves without cracking, underblowing, or leaking a finger.
- Move through the D-major scale and the notes traditional tunes actually use (C natural, occasional F natural, the odd accidental in A-modal tunes) with fingerings that work **on their whistle**.
- Hold the rhythmic identity of a tune type: the three-pulse of a jig, the even running notes of a reel, the dotted lift of a hornpipe, the long line of an air.
- Use ornaments as **rhythmic gestures**, not as printed grace notes. A cut should be too short to name as a pitch. A roll should land on the pulse.
- Learn a phrase by ear, keep it when the page is gone, and breathe in places that do not chop the phrase.
- Vary a tune slightly across repeats, and leave space in a session instead of playing every note in a transcription.

Software can observe pitch, timing, and some ornaments through a microphone. It cannot observe fingers directly unless the user is on camera or on a sensor whistle. Tone quality, “lift,” and good taste are only partly measurable. The product should grade what it can hear, coach what it can infer, and stay quiet about the rest.

### The low D is not a transposed high D

Fingerings match the standard six-hole D whistle, sounded one octave lower. Bottom note is D4, about 294 Hz, just above middle C. A usable range is two octaves, D4 to D6 (about 294–1175 Hz), with the top of the second octave less reliable and very whistle-dependent.

What changes in the body and in the air:

| Dimension | High D habit | Low D reality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air | Faster, smaller stream | More volume, slower speed. High-D air cracks the low whistle into the second octave. |
| Response | Quick | Slower attack. Ornaments must be planned; rushed cuts smear. |
| Hands | Comfortable for most adults | Wider stretch. Leaks on low D and E are the default beginner fault. |
| Warm-up | Pitch settles fast | Pitch rises as the tube warms. A tuner that scolds a cold whistle teaches the wrong lesson. |
| Role in a session | Carries the tune in the fiddle register | Sits under or beside the high instruments. Airs, song airs, and an octave-down setting of a session tune are idiomatic. Dance tunes still matter. |
| Condensation | Annoying | Constant. A “whistle health” check is a real feature, not a gimmick. |

C natural is the note that exposes maker differences. On many whistles the first-octave C natural (C5) is cross-fingered `oxx ooo` or `oxx xox`; half-holing the top hole is the universal fallback and the one that slides. Second-octave C natural (C6) is often a half-hole or something like `oxx xxo`, and it will not match across a Susato, an MK, and a wooden Goldie. A fingering chart with one official C natural is wrong often enough to undermine trust.

## 2. Who it is for

One linear “beginner course” fails this audience. Musical background predicts the **failure mode**, and the failure mode should pick the first month of practice.

### Door A — No instrument, little or no notation

They need sound before symbols. The first wins are: a clear low D, a five-note phrase they can sing back, and a pulse they can tap. Staff notation and ABC are unlocked as memory aids after the ear has the phrase. If the app opens on a stave, this person leaves.

Risks to design against: shame about reading, overload from ornament names, and a repertoire that jumps to reels at session tempo.

### Door B — High-D whistle player

They already have fingerings, tune types, and some ornaments. The transfer problems are physical: too much air pressure, ornaments timed for a short fast tube, a left-hand stretch that leaks, and the assumption that second-octave notes “just work” with the same push. Many can already read whistle tab.

Their course is a **retune**, not a restart. Diagnostic: play low D–A slowly, then a phrase they already know from high D, down the octave. Grade octave stability and leaks, not whether they know what a jig is.

### Door C — Other musicians

This door splits immediately.

| Background | What they bring | What breaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical flute or recorder | Tone awareness, reading, practice habits | Tonguing every note. Flute embouchure habits with no embouchure hole. Vibrato as a default. Treating the printed setting as the piece. Counting instead of feeling the jig’s two groups of three. |
| Classical non-wind (piano, strings, voice) | Reading, rhythm vocabulary | No fipple air. They will believe the fingering chart and under-blow. Theory can become a hiding place from sound. |
| Guitar, bouzouki, bodhrán, singers in sessions | Tunes in the head, session culture, repertoire | Finding the notes on six holes. They often want “the setting Mary plays,” not a graded scale. |
| Uilleann pipes or flute in trad | Ornaments, ear, repertoire | Whistle cuts and rolls are related but not the same gestures. Cran-heavy piping habits need translation. Breath phrasing may already be good. |
| Self-taught by tabs and YouTube | Motivation, a few tunes | Uneven pulse, ornaments as decoration glued on after the fact, no sense of sets. |

A ten-minute placement is enough to choose a door. It should include: match a sung or played pitch, hold low D and the octave D without cracking, finger a short dictated phrase, clap a jig phrase versus a reel phrase, and optionally play anything they already know. People who freeze are Door A. People who play a tune with high-D air are Door B. People who nail rhythm on a clap test and then tongue every whistle note are classical transfers inside Door C.

Do not ask “what is your level?” Level is not predictive. Background plus a one-minute recording is.

## 3. Skill graph

Teach a graph, not a table of contents. Nodes unlock when evidence says so. The same node (a long roll on A, a C-natural phrase in G, breathing in a jig) is reached by different edges.

### Strand 1 — Sound

1. Posture and hand position. Piper’s grip, holes covered with pads, right-hand little finger free to anchor. Low D punishes flat fingers.
2. One note: low D, steady, four beats, no tongue after the start.
3. Octave D on the same fingering, found by air speed, then brought back down on purpose.
4. The staircase: E, F♯, G, A, B, C♯, D′. Each new note is a lift, not a new shape.
5. Leaks and squeaks. The coach distinguishes “finger not sealing” from “too much air” from “audio too messy to say.” That distinction is the whole of early feedback.
6. C natural, both ways, on **this** whistle. Calibration drill, not a chart.
7. F natural when a tune needs it (half-hole). Later.
8. Breathing and phrase. Mark breaths in the tune. Low D runs out of air in places a high D forgives.
9. Tone color on airs: a little air vibrato, used rarely. Dance tunes stay straighter.

### Strand 2 — Time

Tune types are the rhythmic syllabus. Order them by how clearly a beginner can feel the pulse, not by how famous the tune is.

| Type | Meter feel | Why it is in the syllabus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air / song air | Free, phrase-led | Best first musical win on low D. Hides finger slowness. Teaches breath and tone. |
| March | Clear duple | Good for note-changing under a beat. |
| Polka | Fast duple, short | Later. Exposes tonguing habits and uneven fingers. |
| Jig | 6/8, two groups of three | First dance form. The pulse is learnable. Huge repertoire in D and G. |
| Slide | 12/8 | After jigs. Same family, longer line. |
| Reel | Running 4/4 | The session default, and where uneven fingers and late ornaments show. Not week one. |
| Hornpipe | Dotted 4/4 | After reels. Swing is a feel, easy to over-notate. |
| Slip jig | 9/8 | After the player can count three groups without staring. |

Swing, “lift,” and the difference between a dotted hornpipe and a reel should be **heard and copied**, then only lightly symbolized. A metronome is a tool inside a drill, not the aesthetic.

### Strand 3 — Ornament as rhythm

Introduce in this order, each one first as a gesture on a single long note, then inside a tune on the beat:

1. **Cut.** A finger snaps open and shut. The pitch blip is shorter than a hearable note. Placement: on the beat, the melody note speaks just after.
2. **Tap (strike).** A finger below the sounding note flicks shut and open. Pitch dips.
3. **Long roll.** A note of three pulses: cut near the start, tap on a later subdivision. This is the heart of jig and reel rhythm on whistle.
4. **Short roll.** Same idea, less room. Needs clean fingers. Later.
5. **Slide.** Half-hole glide into or out of a note. Idiomatic on low D, and the reason half-holed C natural is worth learning even if the player prefers a cross-fingering for speed.
6. **Triplet.** Three notes in the time of one beat-subdivision, often the “false” triplet that is really a rhythmic flick. Easy to over-explain.
7. **Cran.** Piping ornament. Optional color, not core whistle literacy.

Cuts, taps, and rolls are grading targets. Crans and personal variation are demonstration targets. Do not score taste.

### Strand 4 — Tune learning

The traditional method is ear, phrase, repetition, then a neighbor’s setting. Software should imitate that, with notation as an optional overlay that **fades**.

A tune lesson, whatever the door:

1. Hear the whole tune once, in a simple low-D setting, at a kind tempo.
2. Hear phrase 1. Sing or hum it. The app does not need to grade singing finely; it needs the player to try.
3. Play phrase 1 with fingering help available.
4. Loop. Slow is allowed. Ornaments off, then on.
5. Hide the notation for that phrase (Door B and strong Door C get this sooner).
6. Add phrase 2. Always play from the start of the current chunk, not only the new bar.
7. String a set of two tunes only after each can survive without the page.

“Off the page” is a feature, and it should be the default destination, not a pro mode hidden behind a subscription. Session Whistle already markets notation fading. Treat it as core pedagogy.

### Strand 5 — Session behavior

Late content, but name it early so the product does not accidentally teach “play the transcription at speed.”

- Sets: tune into tune, no stop, a shared key family (D into G is normal; random key jumps are not).
- A setting is a version. The Session will have a dozen ABCs. The player learns one, then hears another.
- Leave space. Not every repeat is fully ornamented.
- Tempo comes from the room. Practice tempos are slower than session tempos on purpose, with a stated target so “slow practice” does not become the only gear.

## 4. Product concepts

Brainstorm, then a cut.

### Concept 1 — The adaptive tutor

Placement, skill graph, daily practice of fifteen minutes, listening feedback, repertoire that unlocks. Closest to Yousician or Simply, rebuilt around trad musicianship. Strength: clear learning loop. Risk: gamified streaks fight the session aesthetic, and generic “note match” feedback feels false on ornaments.

### Concept 2 — The practice desk

No course. A serious player brings ABC or a recording and gets: phrase loop, variable tempo that does not smear ornaments, fingering overlay, breath marks, a tuner that knows the scale, and a record-and-compare. Strength: Door B and experienced Door C will pay for this. Risk: Door A bounces. Windway is already close (browser, ABC import, loop, slow-down, generated band).

### Concept 3 — Ear gym and session simulator

Short call-and-response, “name that tune” from an incipit, play the answer, then a virtual session that keeps going if you drop notes. Strength: matches how trad is actually learned. Risk: needs a lot of curated audio; bad synthesized whistles teach a bad sound.

### Concept 4 — The whistle calibrator

A small tool: which C natural is in tune on this stick, where the octave breaks, how sharp it goes when warm, which notes leak. Then every later exercise uses that profile. Strength: uniquely low-D, high trust. Risk: too narrow to be the whole product. Right as a module inside something larger.

### Concept 5 — Teacher edition

A human teacher assigns a set, marks breaths and ornament choices, listens to a take asynchronously. The software handles the dull grading (pitch, pulse, missed phrase) and the teacher handles taste. Strength: trad is a living lineage; software that replaces the teacher will be resented. Software that saves the teacher the first eight weeks of “cover the holes” will be recommended. Risk: two-sided market, slow.

### Recommended shape

Ship concepts 1, 2, and 4 as one product, with concept 3 as the practice texture inside lessons, and leave concept 5 as an export format (a teacher can open a student’s take) rather than a marketplace in version one.

Name the promise in one sentence: **learn low D the way the music works, starting from the body you actually have.**

What the home screen is:

- Today: one sound drill, one time drill, one phrase of a tune. Ten to twenty minutes.
- Optional desk: open any tune in the book and practice it with the same tools.
- A whistle profile, set once and refreshed when they change instrument.

What it is not:

- A social network.
- A 3,000-tune dump as the thing you see first.
- A subscription gate on the tuner.

| The Session, ABC tools, Anytune-style slow-downers | Repertoire and utilities. | No pedagogy. |

The wedge is not “we also detect pitch.” Session Whistle has moved the table stakes up to confidence-gated ornament grading. Differentiate on:

1. **Low D as the instrument**, including hand-span drills, air-volume versus air-speed, warm-up pitch drift, and condensation.
2. **Three doors** into one skill graph.
3. **A whistle profile** that stores which C-natural fingering is in tune, and uses it in every chart and grade.
4. **Ornament-safe practice audio.** Slowing a recording smears cuts. The player should resynthesize from a structural tune plus a gesture layer, or switch to a slower human take. This is a real technical difference from a generic time-stretch button.
5. **An open core** a teacher can inspect: ABC in, annotated setting out, audio stays on device.

If those five are not the focus, this product is a worse Session Whistle.

## 6. Experience design

### First session, by door

**Door A (about 20 minutes).** Make a sound. Cover all holes. Hold D. See a simple six-hole diagram, not a stave. Call-and-response on D–E–F♯–G. Clap the pulse of a slow air. End by playing the first phrase of a three-phrase air with the diagram, then once with it hidden. No ornament words.

**Door B (about 15 minutes).** Whistle profile: play the scale slowly, app notes where the octave breaks and whether `oxx ooo` C natural is usable. Then one tune they are invited to play “as on the high D, but speak softer.” Feedback is only: cracked octaves, leaked low notes, cuts that became full grace notes. Offer the air-speed drill if cracking is constant.

**Door C, reader (about 20 minutes).** Same sound check as A, shorter, because they get it faster or get frustrated faster. A single explicit drill: play a scale **without tonguing** each note. Then a jig phrase shown in staff **and** as sound, with the instruction that the staff is a map of a recording, not the music. Tongue-detector is a kind early gift to flutists: if onsets are too sharp and too many, say so in one sentence.

**Door C, tune-knower.** Skip the scale sermon. “Hum the first phrase of this jig. Now find it.” Fingering appears only after two failed attempts. This person will forgive a rough tuner and will not forgive being treated as a beginner.

### Feedback ethics

- Abstain when confidence is low. A wrong “you’re sharp” is worse than silence.
- Wait-mode for play-along: the cue stops, then the mic listens. Headphones are the recommended setup; speaker bleed on a low whistle (quiet, breathy) is a false-negative factory.
- Separate “wrong note” from “right note, out of tune” from “right pitch, late” from “ornament ate the note.”
- Never show a scoreboard during an air.
- Warm-up grace: first two minutes of a session, intonation tolerance is wider, and the UI can say the whistle is still settling.

### Representation on screen

Show at most two of these at once, chosen by door and by the drill:

- Six holes, top to bottom, filled or open. Half-hole as a split circle. Second octave marked, not redrawn.
- Letter names.
- Staff, with ABC import rendered by a normal engraver.
- A piano-roll or pulse strip for rhythm drills only.
- The recording waveform with phrase brackets, for ear learning.

Color meaning stays stable: pulse, pitch, ornament. Do not invent a rainbow of encouragement.

### Practice session shape

Every day the app proposes three blocks from due nodes in the graph:

1. Sound (3–5 min). Often octave control or C natural on this whistle.
2. Time or ornament (3–5 min). One gesture, one pulse.
3. Tune (8–12 min). One phrase, or a phrase plus the one before it.

The desk mode ignores the proposal and opens the user’s set list. Both modes write the same progress events, so skipping ahead still fills in evidence.

## 7. Engineering pathways

Three ways to build the same core. The core is not the UI.

### Shared core, regardless of shell

```
tune model  →  practice engine  →  listening pipeline  →  progress
ABC + gestures   what to do now    pitch, onset,         skill-graph
phrase marks     and how to grade  ornament hints        evidence
whistle profile
```

Keep this core in a language that can run on device. Practical choice: Rust or C++ for DSP, compiled to a native library and to WebAssembly, with a thin Swift or TypeScript shell. A pure Python research stack (CREPE, music21) is the right place to **prove** graders offline and the wrong place to ship a practice loop.

### Pathway A — Browser PWA first

Windway shows that a whistle practice player can live in the browser, install, and work offline.

- Web Audio for playback, AudioWorklet for the pitch tracker.
- `abcjs` for notation.
- Mic via `getUserMedia`. Users will deny it, and iOS Safari will suspend audio in annoying ways.
- Latency budget is softer. Fine for phrase-level grading. Tight for a real-time “the note changed and the diagram agrees” loop.

Choose A if the team is web-native and the first release is the practice desk plus ear loops, with grading that happens **on a finished phrase**, not every 20 ms. Ship learning value before chasing native latency.

### Pathway B — Native iOS first

This is what Session Whistle did, for good reasons.

- Lower input latency, a real audio session, on-device models, no upload.
- Wait-mode and headphone detection are easier to make reliable.
- App Store tax and review. Android later, or not.

Choose B if the first release must include live ornament grading. The listening problem is the product, and the browser will fight you.

### Pathway C — Desktop practice station

Bigger notation, a decent USB mic, teacher sitting beside the student. Tauri or a native app around the same core.

Choose C only as a second client. Do not lead with it. The practice that matters happens in kitchens.

### Recommendation

**Pathway A for the desk, ear loops, whistle-profile wizard, and phrase grading. Pathway B only for the live coach, and only after the phrase grader is trusted offline.** One core, two shells, in that order.

Reason: ornament grading that feels magical and is sometimes wrong will define the product’s reputation. Phrase-level grading on a recording the user just made is debuggable. A live cursor that flickers between octaves is not. Session Whistle’s own marketing stresses abstention and wait-mode; that is the scar tissue. Earn the live loop.

## 8. The listening stack

Low D is monophonic, which is the one great mercy. Everything else is harder than a piano app.

### What the mic actually receives

- Fundamental from about 294 Hz (D4) to about 1200 Hz (D6), plus short ornament spikes that can jump a third or a fourth above the written note.
- A strong second harmonic. Naive “loudest peak” trackers jump the octave.
- Breath noise, finger-thump transients, room rumble, and sometimes a backing track if wait-mode was ignored.
- A quiet instrument. The user will be told to use headphones and to get the mic closer than feels normal. Phone mic at arm’s length is a supported mode with wider abstention, not a mode that pretends to hear cuts.

Band-limit the tracker to roughly 250–1600 Hz for note tracking, and run a parallel high-time-resolution contour for gestures. Do not low-pass so hard that cuts disappear.

### Two clocks

| Clock | Hop | Job | Algorithm family |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Live note | 20–40 ms | Tuner, fingering follow, “you are on G” | YIN or pYIN in the audio thread, constrained by the expected scale and the whistle profile |
| Phrase grade | 5–10 ms contour, offline | Ornaments, rhythm, wrong notes | pYIN or a small neural pitch model (CREPE-class or Basic Pitch) on the take, then a gesture parser |

Score-guided tracking is allowed and should be the default whenever the exercise has an expected melody. Open transcription (“what tune is this?”) is a different product and a worse grader. Search-within-expected-note, with a penalty for leaving the scale, removes most octave errors.

Neural models are for the offline pass. They are easier to be proud of and harder to run inside an AudioWorklet without a quantization project. Do not block version one on them. pYIN plus a whistle prior will grade scales and simple phrases. It will not, by itself, grade rolls.

### Ornament parser

Do not quantize the audio into a piano roll and then look for grace notes. Standard note segmentation deletes the evidence: a cut is often 20–50 ms, under the minimum note length.

Parse the pitch contour and the onset energy:

- **Cut candidate:** pitch jumps up at least a semitone and returns, duration under about 70 ms, aligned near an expected strong subdivision.
- **Tap candidate:** pitch dips and returns, same duration budget, usually a tone or a semitone.
- **Roll:** a written long note whose contour contains a cut and a later tap at plausible subdivisions, and whose body is the written pitch.
- **Slide:** continuous pitch movement over more than about 80 ms between two scale notes. Half-hole C naturals live here.
- **Octave crack:** a jump of about 1200 cents that was not asked for, sustained. This is a sound-strand error, not an ornament.
- **Tongue onset:** a spectral burst with no pitch gesture, on every note, in a passage marked legato. Useful for the flutist door. Easy to false-positive on breath noise, so require a pattern across a whole phrase, not one note.

Grade presence, placement relative to the pulse, and whether the gesture stayed short. Do not grade the cent value of the cut. The cut’s pitch is whatever the finger produced.

Confidence: if breath noise or a second sound source makes the contour unstable, mark the phrase “couldn’t hear” and do not count it as a fail. Store the take only on device so a later model can be evaluated; do not upload it by default.

### Rhythm

Dance tunes want a pulse grid from the metronome or from the backing, not from the player’s own onsets (those are what you are measuring). Compare note onsets to the grid with a tolerance that shrinks as the node is mastered. For airs, grade phrase shape and duration, not a grid. An air on a metronome is a category error.

Swing on hornpipes: compare to a template with a swing ratio (around long-short, not a fixed classical double-dot) and allow a wide band. Copying a recording beats matching a ratio.

### Whistle profile

Stored per instrument the user names (“MK low D”, “Susato”, “blackwood”):

- Measured cents offset of each scale note when warm, relative to equal temperament. Low whistles are not equal-tempered in practice; the grader should learn the instrument’s own D scale.
- Preferred C-natural fingering, chosen by which one lands closest and speaks cleanly during calibration.
- Octave-break breath hint: the air level, coarsely, at which D′ speaks. This can be a simple energy threshold from calibration, not a real flow sensor.
- Cold-versus-warm offset, if they run calibration twice. Informational.

Intonation grading uses the profile as the target, equal temperament only as a fallback before calibration. This single decision prevents the app from fighting a whistle that is consistently 15 cents sharp on F♯ and calling the player unskilled.

### What not to sense

- Camera finger tracking. Occlusion, grip styles, and low trust. Revisit only if mic evidence is provably insufficient for a specific drill.
- A custom sensor whistle as a requirement. Interesting for a lab, fatal for a product. Optional MIDI-hole instruments exist in research and should not be the happy path.
- Breath sensors. Infer air faults from pitch jumps, noise, and instability.

## 9. Content model

### Separate the tune from the performance

This split is the main data-model decision.

**Structural tune.** Pitches and durations in ABC (or an internal note list parsed from ABC). Key, type, phrase boundaries, suggested breaths. This is what notation renders and what “wrong note” compares against.

**Gesture layer.** Time-aligned ornaments, slides, and articulation that a player is asked to produce at this stage of the course. Stored beside the ABC, not as a thicket of grace notes inside it. The same jig can have a bare gesture layer for month one and a rolled layer for month four.

**Takes.** Audio the user played, on device, plus the parser’s events.

Why: ABC grace-note spelling is not standardized enough to grade against. One transcriber writes a cut as a `{g}`, another absorbs it into a roll symbol, another leaves the tune bare. If you grade against raw Session ABC you will punish traditional playing and reward literal grace notes.

### Playback that survives slow-down

Three legal ways to play a model phrase slower:

1. **Human takes at two or three tempi**, phrase-aligned. Best sound. Costs money and time. Do this for the core syllabus (on the order of 30–40 tunes, not thousands).
2. **Resynthesis from structural notes + gesture layer**, using a simple sampled whistle or a waveguide model, triggering cut and tap samples as events. Ornaments stay short at any tempo because they are events, not stretched audio. Good enough for desk mode on imported ABC.
3. **Time-stretch of a recording.** Acceptable for airs and for listening to a source recording. Bad for dance-tune ornaments. If you ship a slow-down knob on recordings, do not also grade the user’s ornaments against the smeared audio.

Generated “band” (bodhrán, a chording instrument) should be synthesized from the tune type and chord hints, at the practice tempo, and it must duck or stop in wait-mode. Do not put pipes on every tune by default; a low D buried under a synthesized pipe channel teaches people to overblow.

### Repertoire plan

Curate. Do not lead with the whole of The Session.

| Stage | Material | Keys on a D whistle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sound | Original micro-etudes: long notes, hole lifts, octave pops, C-natural switches | D |
| First tunes | Airs and slow marches in D, range of a fifth, no C natural | D |
| Jigs | Well-known, legally usable settings in D, then G (introduces C natural) | D, G |
| Reels | After pulse is stable. D, then E minor / A modal | D, Em, A |
| Sets | Two tunes, related keys, one breath between | — |
| Desk | Anything the user imports | Prefer D, G, A, Em, Bm; warn on hostile keys |

A D whistle is at home in D major, G major, A mixolydian / A dorian, E dorian, B minor. A tune in F is a novelty, not a lesson. Say so when an import is in a hostile key, and offer to transpose only when the result still fits the instrument. Blind transpose-to-D of a setting full of awkward accidentals is how people learn to hate C natural.

Cross-fingerings and half-holes should appear first inside a tune that needs them (a G-major jig), not as a scale of death.

### Licensing

[The Session](https://thesession.org/api) has a read-only API and weekly CSV/JSON dumps. The database is under the Open Database License. That is usable and it is not “free music, do what you like.”

- Attribute The Session, keep ODbL obligations if you republish a substantial part of the database.
- Prefer the bulk dumps plus your own indexing over live scraping.
- Treat individual tune settings as something you **select and annotate**, not as a mirror of the site. Many products ship “community tunes”; the pedagogical value is the annotation (phrases, breaths, gesture layers, teaching order), which you should own.
- Record original syllabus audio. Do not ship other people’s commercial recordings.
- ABC import is the escape valve for everything you do not curate.

Commission a small set of settings from a player who is comfortable teaching low D, and pay for a written license that allows slow-down, phrase slicing, and resynthesis. Thirty well-taught tunes will beat three thousand bare ABCs for the course. The desk can still open the long tail.

## 10. System shape

### Suggested stack for the first build

- **Client:** TypeScript, a small UI, `abcjs`, Web Audio, AudioWorklet pitch worker compiled from a shared DSP library.
- **DSP library:** Rust (or C) with YIN/pYIN, onset energy, and the gesture parser. WASM in the browser. The same crate later links into an iOS static lib.
- **Content pipeline:** a Python toolbox that is not shipped. Parse ABC (music21 or a focused ABC parser), align phrases, generate gesture-layer templates, batch-evaluate the grader against labeled takes. This is where CREPE or Basic Pitch may be used as a reference pitch track to compare your pYIN against.
- **Service:** boring. Auth optional. Serve the syllabus and the tune index. Store progress JSON. Do not store audio unless a user explicitly sends a take for support, and delete it on a short timer.
- **Offline:** the current tune book and the DSP worker cache locally. A kitchen with bad data is a primary venue.

### Practice engine

A skill node has:

- Prerequisites.
- A probe: the exercise definition (expected notes, gesture layer, tempo, tolerances).
- A mastery rule: for example, four clean takes out of the last six, on at least two different days.
- A decay rule: nodes that depend on the body (octave, C natural, long roll) come back if later evidence shows they slipped.

The daily selector picks one due node from sound, one from time or ornament, and continues the active tune. It should be deterministic and explainable (“C natural came back because the G jig keeps dragging that note sharp”), not a black-box recommender.

### Import path

User pastes ABC. You accept it only if it parses, fits a plausible whistle range after an optional transpose into a friendly key, and you can build a note list. Reject with a specific reason (“this setting goes below low D”, “could not find a key”, “too many accidentals for a D whistle”). Session Whistle’s “accepted only when the tune can actually play” rule is the right one. Steal it.

Then auto-build a **bare** gesture layer (no ornaments) and let the user toggle a “traditional suggestions” layer. Suggestions are rules of thumb (long notes on the beat in jigs often take a roll; phrase starts often take a cut), marked as suggestions, never as the correct answer.

## 11. Phased build

### Phase 0 — Listening lab (2–4 weeks, not a release)

Record a small corpus on two or three low Ds: scales, octave cracks, leaked notes, cuts, taps, rolls, slides, tongued versus legato, cold versus warm, phone mic versus close mic, with and without a bodhrán in the room. Label by hand.

Success: a written error analysis. Where does pYIN octave-jump, where do cuts vanish, what false-positive rate do rolls have. If rolls are not detectable at close-mic with abstention on the rest, the live ornament coach stays off the roadmap until the corpus says otherwise. Do not skip this phase because an existing app claims the feature.

### Phase 1 — Desk and sound (first public release)

- Whistle profile calibration.
- Six-hole diagram, staff via ABC, letter names.
- Tuner with profile target and warm-up grace.
- Phrase loop, metronome, tempo change.
- Playback from multi-tempo human takes for the small syllabus, resynthesis for imports.
- Phrase-level grader for **pitch and pulse only**, with abstention.
- Three doors that change copy, order, and which view opens, even if the grader is still simple.
- One air, two jigs, one reel, all in friendly keys, fully phrased.

No ornament grades yet. The ornament **lessons** can exist as listen-and-copy with a human recording. Grading them before the lab supports it is how you get one-star reviews from good players.

### Phase 2 — Gestures and the graph

- Offline gesture parser on user takes.
- Grades for cut, tap, long roll, unwanted octave crack, chronic tonguing.
- Skill graph with mastery and decay.
- Notation fade.
- Set practice: two tunes, backing that stops when it is the user’s turn.
- Syllabus grows toward a few dozen tunes.

### Phase 3 — Live coach, probably native

- AudioWorklet or iOS audio unit, expected-note tracker, fingering highlight that follows.
- Wait-mode play-along.
- Only the gestures phase 2 already grades reliably offline.
- Android if the iOS loop is the one people actually use.

### Phase 4 — Optional, only with a reason

- Teacher share links (a take plus the parser trace, explicit upload).
- Name-that-tune from the on-device index.
- More instruments. Do not do this early. Low D focus is the point.

## 12. Risks

| Risk | Why it bites | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ornament grader is confidently wrong | Trad players will test a roll in the first minute and then distrust every pitch arrow. | Phase 0 corpus. Abstain. Ship copy-the-recording before grades. |
| High-D app with a transpose button | Does not fix air, hands, or C natural. | Whistle profile and Door B are release criteria, not backlog. |
| Repertoire copyright and ODbL | A tune dump feels fastest and is the legally messiest part. | Curated licensed audio. ABC import. ODbL dumps for search, with attribution. |
| Synthesized whistle as the model sound | Students imitate a bad attack. | Human takes for anything you ask them to copy. |
| One course for all doors | Classical players quit because it is slow; beginners quit because it assumes note names. | Placement is part of phase 1, not a personalization research project. |
| Streaks and points | Insulting to the session culture you claim to serve. Light progress is enough. | Show “phrases that survive off the page,” not a combo meter. |
| Mic theater | Grading speaker bleed, or a TV in the room. | Wait-mode, headphone prompt, abstention. |
| Hand size | Some adults cannot seal a large low D. Software cannot stretch a hand. | Say so in onboarding. Do not imply practice will fix a whistle that is too big. Mention smaller-holed or mid-price polymer low Ds as the physical prerequisite, without turning the app into a shop. |
| Warm-up scolding | The best players look “out of tune” for the first minutes. | Profile plus grace period. |

## 13. Open decisions

These are worth a human answer before a build locks them.

1. **Who is the first door?** Door B (high-D players moving down) is the smallest curriculum and the sharpest wedge, and they already own the instrument. Door A is the larger market and the longer course. A phase-1 release can onboard all three and write deep content for one.
2. **Is live grading the identity of the product?** If yes, pathway B and phase 0 dominate the schedule. If the identity is “the low-D practice desk that teaches in the right order,” pathway A ships sooner and the microphone is a phrase recorder.
3. **Single whistle key forever?** Low D only is a clearer product. “Any D whistle, with a low-D course” is honest, because fingerings match and high-D owners are the feeder. Supporting C and B♭ whistles doubles fingering tests and splits the ear repertoire. Recommend: D fingering only, low-D course, a profile flag for high D so Door B’s air targets change.
4. **Who plays the model takes?** One consistent player is better pedagogy than a compilation. Budget for a low-D player who articulates cleanly at slow tempi, not only a session star at full speed.
5. **Accounts.** Progress sync is convenient and audio sync is a liability. Default local. Account optional, and only for the syllabus position and whistle profile.

## 14. Recommendation

Build a low-D practice teacher whose first milestone does not grade ornaments.

1. Record a labeled corpus on real low Ds and decide, with numbers, which faults are hearable.
2. Ship a browser practice desk: whistle calibration, three onboarding doors, ABC that must be playable, human model phrases, phrase loop, pitch-and-pulse grading that would rather say nothing than say the wrong thing.
3. Put the skill graph under that desk so a non-reader, a high-D player, and a flutist do not see the same first week.
4. Add cut, tap, and roll grades only when the corpus says the parser abstains honestly.
5. Move the live coach to native code when offline phrase grades are already trusted.

The musical bet is that low D is its own instrument: more air, slower speech, wider hands, a C natural that depends on the stick, and a repertoire of airs and dance tunes learned by phrase. The engineering bet is that a gesture layer plus a confidence-gated contour parser will teach that instrument, and that a transposed high-D tuner will not.
